The balanced plate, adapted for diabetes

6 min read · Last updated 3 June 2026

Counting calories at every meal is exhausting, and almost nobody keeps it up for life. Fortunately there is a simpler method that works nearly as well: manage the proportions on the plate instead of the numbers.

How the plate divides

Indonesia's Isi Piringku guidance from the Ministry of Health splits a single meal like this:

  • Half the plate, vegetables and fruit. Two parts vegetables, one part fruit, favouring non-starchy vegetables: spinach, water spinach, green beans, mustard greens, aubergine, chayote.
  • A quarter, protein. Fish, skinless chicken, eggs, tempeh, tofu, or legumes. Alternate animal and plant sources through the day.
  • A quarter, carbohydrate. Rice, potato, sweetcorn, sweet potato, or sago. Note: a quarter, not a half. This is the portion that most often creeps.

Round it out with water (about eight glasses a day), 30 minutes of activity, and weighing yourself regularly.

What changes for diabetes: the order and the carbohydrate portion.

The plate rule is the same for everyone. Three small adjustments make it work harder for blood sugar: keep carbohydrate to a quarter, eat fruit whole rather than juiced, and start the meal with vegetables or protein before the rice.

Translating it to a real plate

Real meals rarely arrive neatly separated on a flat plate. A few practical adjustments:

  1. One scoop of rice, not as much as you fancy. A heaped scoop is closer to two portions. Take one, then fill the plate with vegetables until it looks full.
  2. For soups and one-bowl dishes, count what is inside. Soto, meatball soup, or gado-gado: estimate the rice cake, noodles, or vermicelli, that is your carbohydrate share.
  3. Noodles and rice are not a pair. Both are carbohydrate. Pick one and spend the rest on protein and vegetables.
  4. Fritters are not a protein dish. Fried tempeh is still tempeh, but vegetable fritters are closer to carbohydrate plus oil.
  5. Fruit as dessert, one portion. A slice of papaya, one orange, or one small banana, not a full fruit salad.

If the portion still feels small

That feeling usually comes from volume, not calories. Add vegetables (boiled, lightly stir-fried, or as a clear soup) until the plate is genuinely full. Their fibre and water fill you up without raising blood sugar the way extra rice does.

Needs differ: height, activity level, medication, and conditions such as kidney disease all shift the right numbers. If a dietitian has given you a specific plan, that plan wins; this plate is the general frame.
